OKRs (Objectives & Key Results) originated at Intel and were popularized by Google. Hoshin Kanri ("policy deployment") originated in 1960s Japan. Both solve the same problem — connecting daily work to enterprise strategy — but with different philosophies. Many mature organizations use both.
01 · Side-by-Side Comparison
OKRs
  • ›Objective: qualitative, inspiring goal
  • ›Key Results: 3-5 measurable outcomes
  • ›Quarterly cadence + annual goals
  • ›Bidirectional — top-down AND bottom-up
  • ›70% achievement = success on stretch goals
  • ›Best for: Google, Spotify, LinkedIn culture
  • ›Tools: Lattice, Workboard, Asana
Hoshin Kanri
  • ›Breakthrough objective: 1-3 year vision
  • ›Annual goals cascade to departments
  • ›Annual planning + monthly reviews
  • ›"Catchball" — negotiated top-down alignment
  • ›Near 100% achievement expected
  • ›Best for: Toyota, Danaher, HP, manufacturing
  • ›Tools: X-Matrix, A3 reports, Bowling chart
02 · Key Concepts
🎾
Catchball
Hoshin goals are "thrown" down from leadership and "caught" at each level. Teams push back with capacity constraints and negotiate achievable targets — creating commitment, not compliance.
Negotiate → Commit → Execute
🏆
Stretch Goals (OKRs)
OKRs are intentionally ambitious. Google's rule: if you hit 100% of your OKRs, they weren't ambitious enough. 70% on a stretch goal beats 100% on a safe one.
60-70% achievement = success
📊
X-Matrix (Hoshin)
A single-page visual that maps: strategic goals → annual priorities → projects → owners → KPIs. Shows exactly how everything connects across the organization.
One page shows everything
🔁
PDCA Integration
Hoshin runs on the Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle. Annual planning = Plan; execution = Do; monthly reviews = Check; adjustments = Act. OKRs use a similar quarterly review cadence.
Plan → Do → Check → Act
03 · Framework Comparison
DimensionOKRsHoshin Kanri
PhilosophyAchieve ambitious outcomesDeploy strategy with precision
CycleQuarterly + AnnualAnnual + Monthly reviews
DirectionBidirectional (top-down + bottom-up)Top-down with negotiation

WeaknessCan become disconnected activitiesCan become bureaucratic
Key Takeaway
OKRs work best in environments valuing autonomy and experimentation; Hoshin where precision and perfect deployment matter. Use OKRs for innovation bets and Hoshin for operational excellence — they complement each other beautifully.

Most professionals write emails and presentations in the order they think — background first, then findings, then recommendation. Executives need the opposite: answer first, supporting arguments second. This is the Pyramid Principle, developed by McKinsey consultant Barbara Minto in the 1970s.
01 · The Pyramid Principle
Recommendation / Answer
State your conclusion FIRST — before any supporting information
Argument 1 · Argument 2 · Argument 3
3-5 parallel arguments that prove the recommendation
Supporting Detail 1a, 1b, 1c / 2a, 2b / 3a, 3b
Data, examples, evidence that supports each argument
02 · The SCQA Framework
📍
Situation
Establish the context everyone agrees on. What's the current state? Keep it brief — shared facts only.
What is true now
⚡
Complication
What has changed or what is the tension? This is what disrupts the comfortable situation and creates the need for action.
What changed / the problem
❓
Question
What question does the complication raise? This is the question your document answers. Makes the reader lean forward.
What we need to decide
💡
Answer
Your recommendation, stated clearly upfront. The rest of the document proves why this answer is correct.
Your recommendation
03 · Common Executive Communication Mistakes
❌ What Most People Do
  • ›Start with background and history
  • ›Bury the recommendation on page 5
  • ›Use jargon and acronyms freely
  • ›Build to the point (journey narrative)
  • ›Show all your work and analysis
  • ›Present options without a recommendation
  • ›Use dense text paragraphs
✓ What Works
  • ›State the recommendation in sentence one
  • ›Support it with 3 parallel arguments
  • ›Use plain language; define terms used
  • ›Lead with the point (executive narrative)
  • ›Include only analysis that supports the ask
  • ›Always recommend — executives decide, you lead
  • ›Use structured visuals: tables, bullets, charts
04 · Data Visualization Principles
📊
Show the insight, not the data
Don't present a table and let the reader find the trend. Highlight it. Write the headline AS the chart title: "Fraud losses increased 40% in Q3" not "Quarterly Fraud Losses."
🎯
One chart, one message
Each visual should make exactly one point. If you're tempted to put two trends on one chart, that's two slides. The reader's eye can only follow one story.
📐
Match chart type to data type
Trend over time → line chart. Comparing categories → bar chart. Part of whole → stacked bar (not pie). Relationship between variables → scatter plot.
🔴
Use color intentionally
Red = bad/decline. Green = good/growth. Use sparingly — maximum 3 colors. Never use color as decoration. Ensure colorblind accessibility.
Key Takeaway
The Pyramid Principle transforms communication from an exercise in sharing information to an exercise in making decisions easier. When your audience receives your answer first, they can evaluate your logic rather than wondering where you're going. This respects their time and builds trust.

Change management is the structured approach to transitioning individuals, teams, and organizations from a current state to a desired future state. Three models dominate the field: Kotter's 8-Step Process (organizational), ADKAR (individual), and the Kübler-Ross Change Curve (psychological). Each answers a different question about change.
01 · Kotter's 8-Step Model (Organizational Change)
Step 1
Create Urgency
Help others see the need for change and the importance of acting immediately. Without urgency, people stay comfortable.
Step 2
Build a Guiding Coalition
Assemble a group with enough power, credibility, and diversity to lead the change effort.
Step 3
Form a Strategic Vision
Create a vision and strategy that shows how the future will be different and how change will make that future a reality.
Step 4
Communicate the Vision
Communicate the change vision relentlessly. Use every vehicle possible. Lead by example.
Step 5
Enable Action by Removing Barriers
Remove obstacles to change: structures, systems, people, and processes that block implementation.
Step 6
Generate Short-Term Wins
Plan for and create visible, unambiguous successes as soon as possible. Celebrate wins.
Step 7
Sustain Acceleration
Use momentum to tackle bigger change. Don't declare victory too early.
Step 8
Institute the Change
Anchor change in culture. Show how new behaviors and approaches delivered success.
02 · ADKAR Model (Individual Change)
💡
Awareness
Does the person understand WHY change is needed? What is the risk of not changing? Without awareness, all else fails.
A — Why change?
❤️
Desire
Does the person WANT to participate in and support the change? Desire is personal. It cannot be mandated — only influenced.
D — Want to change?
📚
Knowledge
Does the person KNOW how to change? Training, documentation, and coaching address this. Knowledge without desire = wasted training.
K — Know how to change?
⚙️
Ability
Can the person actually IMPLEMENT the change? This is where most training programs fail — knowledge ≠ ability. Practice matters.
A — Can actually do it?
🔒
Reinforcement
Are there systems in place to SUSTAIN the change? Without reinforcement, people revert. Recognition, consequences, and measurement.
R — Will it stick?
03 · The Change Curve (Kübler-Ross Applied)
StageEmotional StateWhat People NeedManager Action
Shock/DenialDisbelief, numbnessClear informationCommunicate facts, allow processing time
Anger/FearResistance, anxietyTo be heardListen actively, acknowledge concerns
Bargaining"What if we…?" negotiatingInvolvementInclude in problem-solving, give some control
DepressionLow energy, disconnectionSupport, not pressureCheck in frequently, celebrate small wins
Acceptance"OK, let's make it work"Tools and directionProvide training, clear next steps
CommitmentActive engagementOwnershipDelegate, recognize contributions, sustain momentum
Key Takeaway
Most change failures are people failures, not strategy failures. Kotter tells you what to do organizationally; ADKAR tells you what each individual needs; the change curve tells you where someone is emotionally. Use all three together — the model that accounts for emotions will succeed where rational models fail.

An LLM (Large Language Model) is a type of AI trained on enormous amounts of text to predict the most likely next token in a sequence. From this simple task, it learns grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, and language nuance. The full HTML infographic is available separately.
01 · The AI Spectrum
🎯
Narrow AI (today)
AI designed for one specific task. Cannot transfer skills between domains. Examples: spam filters, fraud detection, image recognition, recommendation engines.
🧠
General AI / AGI (frontier)
AI that can reason across domains, transfer knowledge, and handle novel situations. LLMs approach this but aren't truly there yet.
Current frontier: GPT-4, Claude, Gemini
🚀
Super AI (theoretical)
AI surpassing human intelligence in every domain simultaneously. Purely theoretical — not built or demonstrated.
Science fiction for now

03 · Key Concepts
📏
Context Window
Maximum text an LLM can "see" at once. GPT-4 Turbo: 128K tokens (~96K words). Larger = more capable but more expensive.
Like short-term memory
🌡️
Temperature
Controls randomness. Low (0.1) = predictable, factual. High (1.0) = creative, varied. Most apps use 0.3-0.7.
0.0 deterministic → 1.0 creative
⚠️

LLMs generate confident-sounding but factually wrong information. Critical risk for financial and compliance uses. Always verify important outputs.
Key risk in regulated industries

Retrieve → Ground → Generate
Key Takeaway
LLMs are not search engines or databases. They are pattern-completion systems trained to predict plausible text. Their power comes from the breadth of patterns learned; their risk comes from generating plausible-but-wrong outputs with equal confidence. In financial services, human review of LLM outputs is essential for high-stakes decisions.

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting systems, networks, and data from digital attacks. For financial institutions, it is consistently the #1 technology risk — banks are among the most targeted organizations on earth. Understanding the threat landscape is essential for second-line oversight of technology risk.
01 · Major Threat Categories
🎣
Social Engineering / Phishing
Manipulating humans to reveal credentials or click malicious links. Accounts for 80%+ of breaches. Variants: spear phishing (targeted), vishing (voice), smishing (SMS), BEC (business email compromise).
#1 attack vector
🔐
Ransomware
Malware that encrypts systems and demands payment. Banks are prime targets — high payment ability and operational dependency. Colonial Pipeline and dozens of financial institutions hit annually.
Average demand: $1.5M+
👤
Insider Threats
Malicious or negligent employees, contractors, or vendors. Particularly damaging in banks due to privileged access to customer data and financial systems. Harder to detect than external threats.
Often undetected for months
🌐
Third-Party / Supply Chain
Attacks through vendor relationships. SolarWinds, Log4j, MOVEit. A bank's risk posture is only as strong as its weakest third-party vendor with system access.
75% of data exposures involve 3rd parties

04 · Zero Trust Principles
🚫
Never Trust, Always Verify
No implicit trust based on network location. Every access request is authenticated and authorized, regardless of whether it comes from inside or outside the perimeter.
Core principle
🔒
Least Privilege Access
Grant only the minimum access required for the task, for the minimum time required. JIT (Just-in-Time) provisioning eliminates standing privileges.
Minimize blast radius
📋
Assume Breach
Design controls assuming the attacker is already inside. Micro-segmentation, behavioral analytics, and rapid detection/response matter as much as prevention.
Limit lateral movement
Key Takeaway
Defense-in-depth and zero trust are complementary philosophies: defense-in-depth layers controls so that no single failure is catastrophic; zero trust ensures that even authorized users are continuously verified. Neither prevents all attacks — the goal is rapid detection, containment, and recovery.

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy
Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) is one of the most evidence-based forms of psychotherapy. Developed by Aaron Beck in the 1960s, it is based on the insight that our thoughts (cognitions) — not events themselves — drive our feelings and behaviors. By identifying and changing unhelpful thought patterns, we can change how we feel and act.
01 · The Cognitive Triangle
💭
Thoughts (Cognitions)
"I can't do this." "Everyone thinks I'm incompetent." "This meeting is going to go terribly." Automatic thoughts are fast, often unconscious, and frequently distorted.
The driver of the cycle
❤️
Feelings (Emotions)
Anxiety, sadness, anger, shame. Emotions arise from our thoughts about events — not the events themselves. Two people in the same situation feel differently because their thoughts differ.
Driven by thoughts
🏃
Behaviors (Actions)
Avoidance, procrastination, lashing out, overworking. Behaviors are responses to feelings, which were triggered by thoughts. Changing the thought breaks the cycle.
Driven by feelings
02 · The 10 Cognitive Distortions
🔲
All-or-Nothing Thinking
"This presentation was a disaster." Seeing things in black and white, with no middle ground. "If I'm not perfect, I'm a failure."
⚡
Catastrophizing
"If I make one mistake, I'll lose my job." Assuming the worst possible outcome. Also: minimizing positives (the flip side).
🔮
Mind Reading
"I know they think I'm not ready for this." Assuming you know what others are thinking, usually negatively, with no evidence.
🏷️
Labeling
"I'm an idiot." Attaching a global negative label to yourself or others based on one event, rather than describing the specific behavior.
👆
Personalization
"The meeting went badly because of me." Taking excessive responsibility for negative events outside your control.
🔍
Mental Filter
Focusing exclusively on one negative detail while ignoring the full picture. One critical comment ruins 10 positive ones.

02 · The Vagus Nerve — Your Regulation Pathway
🧬
What is the Vagus Nerve?
The longest cranial nerve, running from the brainstem to the abdomen. The primary channel of the parasympathetic nervous system. High vagal tone = better stress resilience, emotional regulation, and heart rate variability.
Higher vagal tone = more resilient
🌬️
Why Slow Breathing Works
Exhalation activates the vagus nerve. Longer exhales than inhales directly stimulate parasympathetic response. This is why 4-7-8 breathing (inhale 4, hold 7, exhale 8) works so quickly — you are directly activating the "off switch."
Exhale activates vagus nerve

📦
Box Breathing (Navy SEALs)
Inhale 4 counts → Hold 4 → Exhale 4 → Hold 4. Used by military, emergency services, and athletes. Equalizes sympathetic/parasympathetic activation. Best for: high-stress preparation, before difficult conversations.
4-4-4-4
💤
4-7-8 Breathing (Sleep)

4-7-8

Double inhale through the nose (short inhale + second sniff to fully inflate lungs) → long slow exhale. Pops collapsed alveoli and rapidly resets CO2/O2 ratio. Takes 30 seconds. Best for: immediate stress reduction.
Quickest reset: 30 seconds
❤️
Coherent Breathing (HRV)
Breathe at exactly 5-6 breaths per minute (5 second inhale, 5 second exhale). Maximizes heart rate variability (HRV), a marker of cardiovascular health and resilience. Best for: daily practice, building baseline resilience.
5 breaths/minute = peak HRV

Harder in the moment = better long-term

Test of Design vs Test of Effectiveness

TOE

Both

Control testing is how the second line (and audit) provides assurance that controls are actually working — not just documented on paper. Every material control should be tested both for Design (would this control prevent or detect the risk if properly executed?) and Effectiveness (is the control actually being executed as designed, consistently, over time?).
01 · TOD vs TOE Explained
TOD — Test of Design
  • ›Is the control designed to mitigate the risk?
  • ›Would it work if properly executed?
  • ›Looks at WHAT the control is, not how often it ran
  • ›Methods: walkthrough, inquiry, inspection of design
  • ›Produces: design opinion (adequate / inadequate)
  • ›When: before testing effectiveness; new controls; after redesign
  • ›Timing: can be done at one point in time
TOE — Test of Effectiveness
  • ›Is the control actually working, consistently, over time?
  • ›Is it being performed as designed, every time?
  • ›Looks at HOW the control is operating in practice
  • ›Methods: sampling, inspection of evidence, re-performance
  • ›Produces: operating effectiveness opinion
  • ›When: for all key controls, annually at minimum
  • ›Timing: requires population/sample over a period

A policy framework is the formal structure that defines how an organization governs risk and operations through documented requirements. Without a coherent hierarchy, organizations end up with overlapping, contradictory, or outdated documents that no one reads. A well-designed policy pyramid ensures every document has a purpose, owner, and audience.

Procedures
How to perform processes that implement standards. Process-level detail with roles and steps.

Step-by-step task-level instructions. Most granular. Written for the individual performing the task.

Core control design attributes
A control is any action, process, or mechanism designed to prevent, detect, or correct a risk. Control design is the discipline of ensuring that the right controls exist, are designed to actually work, and are implemented in a way that is both effective and sustainable. Poor control design is one of the most common root causes of audit findings.
01 · Control Types by Objective
🛡️
Preventive Controls
Stop a risk event from occurring in the first place. Generally more effective and less costly than detective controls. Examples: access controls, segregation of duties, approval workflows, system edit checks.
Stop it before it happens
🔍
Detective Controls
Identify when a risk event has occurred, so it can be investigated and corrected. Examples: reconciliations, exception reports, audit trails, variance analysis, monitoring dashboards.
Find it after it happens
🔧
Corrective Controls
Restore normal operations after a risk event is detected. Examples: backup and recovery procedures, incident response plans, remediation procedures, insurance.
Fix it once found
02 · Control Modality
Manual Controls
  • ›Performed by humans
  • ›Susceptible to human error and inconsistency
  • ›Can be overridden by people with access
  • ›Test by sampling — verify evidence of execution
  • ›Higher testing burden (need more samples)
  • ›More flexible — humans can exercise judgment
  • ›Example: manager review and approval of transactions
Automated Controls
  • ›Performed by systems, automatically, every time
  • ›Consistent — no human error variance
  • ›Cannot be overridden without system access change
  • ›Test by verifying system configuration still set correctly
  • ›Lower testing burden — test configuration once
  • ›Less flexible — handles standard cases only
  • ›Example: system blocks payment over credit limit
03 · Five Core Control Design Attributes
🎯
Precision
The control fires exactly when the risk condition exists and not otherwise. Low precision = false positives (too sensitive) or missed events (not sensitive enough).
Right signal, right time
📏
Coverage
What percentage of the risk population does the control address? A control that only applies to transactions over $1M misses all transactions under that threshold.
What % of the risk does it cover?
⚙️
Sustainability
Can the control be performed consistently over time without degradation? Manual controls performed by one person are not sustainable if that person leaves.
Will it still work next year?
📝
Documentability
Can evidence be produced that the control was performed? For SOX and regulatory purposes, if there's no evidence, it didn't happen.
No evidence = didn't happen
🔗
Traceability
Can the control be linked to a specific risk it mitigates? Controls without a clear risk linkage often indicate either orphaned controls or inadequate risk assessment.
Each control links to a risk
04 · Key vs Non-Key Controls
Key Controls
  • ›Directly mitigate material risks
  • ›Required to be tested annually
  • ›Findings on key controls escalate to senior management
  • ›Included in regulatory reporting
  • ›Failure has material impact potential
  • ›Small number — often 20-30% of control population
Non-Key Controls
  • ›Support process efficiency, not direct risk mitigation
  • ›Tested less frequently or via management self-assessment
  • ›Findings managed within the business
  • ›Informational for internal purposes
  • ›Failure has limited direct risk impact
  • ›Majority of control population — often 70-80%
Key Takeaway
The most common control design failure: controls designed for compliance theater rather than actual risk mitigation. A control that requires a manager to "review and approve" a report without any guidance on what to look for, how to document exceptions, or what constitutes a problem is a control in name only. Effective control design starts with the risk and works backward to the mechanism — not the other way around.

Control testing is the systematic process of providing assurance that controls are designed appropriately (TOD) and operating effectively (TOE). Unlike external audit which tests controls once annually, second-line control testing should be risk-based, continuous, and forward-looking — identifying control failures before they become regulatory findings.
01 · End-to-End Testing Lifecycle
Phase 1
Planning
Define scope: which controls to test, test approach (TOD/TOE/both), testing period, sampling methodology, resource requirements. Risk tier your control population — high-risk controls tested more frequently and with larger samples.
Phase 2
Evidence Collection
Obtain evidence that the control was performed. Evidence types: (1) system-generated (most reliable — logs, exception reports), (2) documented approvals (email, system timestamps), (3) reconciliations and tie-outs, (4) interview notes. Missing evidence = exception.
Phase 3
Analysis & Testing
For TOD: walk through control design against risk scenario. For TOE: review each sample item against defined control attributes. Document exception for any item that does not meet the standard. Exception ≠ automatic finding — assess severity.
Phase 4
Findings & Reporting
Classify exceptions as deficiencies or material weaknesses. Draft findings with: (1) condition (what was found), (2) criteria (what should be), (3) cause (root cause), (4) effect (risk impact), (5) recommendation (what to fix). Issue formal report.
Phase 5
Remediation Tracking
Management provides remediation plan with owner and target date. Second line tracks to closure. Validates remediation is sustainable (not a one-time fix). Re-tests in next cycle to confirm resolution.

04 · Compensating Controls
🔄
What is a Compensating Control?
An alternative control that mitigates the risk that the excepted policy was designed to address. A compensating control must actually address the same risk — not simply document that the exception exists. "We have a policy that says..." is not a compensating control.
Must address the same risk
⚠️
Exception Accumulation Risk
Organizations often manage individual exceptions without seeing the population. 50 low-risk exceptions in the same control domain may indicate a systemic policy design problem. Exception register analytics — by domain, by age, by risk level — provide this visibility.
Review population, not just individual items

Regulators look at exception populations as leading indicators of control culture. A small number of documented, time-bound exceptions with genuine compensating controls indicates mature risk management. A large, aged exception population with automatic renewals and no compensating controls indicates a culture where policy requirements are treated as optional — a finding category that escalates quickly.

Issue management is the structured process for identifying, tracking, escalating, and remediating control failures, policy violations, and regulatory findings. Effective issue management is itself an examiner focus area — banks with mature issue management processes demonstrate risk management culture; banks with accumulating aged, poorly remediated issues face enforcement action.
01 · Issue Lifecycle
Step 1
Issue Identification
Source: control testing, self-identified, audit finding, regulatory finding, incident, or complaint. Every issue must be formally logged regardless of source. Self-identified issues (vs regulator-found) are viewed favorably by examiners.
Step 2
Root Cause Analysis
The cause of the issue (not just the symptom). Root cause categories: (1) process design deficiency, (2) operating effectiveness failure, (3) people/skill gap, (4) system failure, (5) governance/oversight gap. Root cause determines sustainable remediation.
Step 3
Remediation Planning
SMART plan: Specific (exactly what will be done), Measurable (how we'll know it's fixed), Achievable (realistic with available resources), Relevant (addresses root cause, not symptom), Time-bound (target date with milestone dates).
Step 4
Remediation Execution
First line executes. Second line monitors. Key risk: first line makes cosmetic changes that satisfy the finding description without fixing the underlying cause. Second line must understand the root cause well enough to evaluate whether the fix is genuine.
Step 5
Closure Validation
Second line (or audit for audit findings) validates that: (1) the stated remediation was actually completed, (2) the evidence provided is genuine and sufficient, (3) the root cause was addressed, (4) the control now operates effectively. Point-in-time evidence is insufficient for process control issues.

03 · Signs of Mature vs Immature Issue Management
Mature Issue Management
  • ›Issues self-identified as often as externally identified
  • ›Root cause analysis is analytical and specific
  • ›Remediation plans address root cause, not symptoms
  • ›Closure validated with evidence, not management representation
  • ›Issue database shows trend analysis and thematic patterns
  • ›Past-due issues are escalated and have management attention
Immature Issue Management
  • ›Most issues discovered by audit or regulators
  • ›Root cause is generic: "lack of oversight" or "process failure"
  • ›Remediation is documentation-only: "policy updated"
  • ›Issues closed based on management's assertion it's fixed
  • ›No trend analysis — issues managed individually
  • ›Large aged population with automatic target date extensions
Key Takeaway
Examiners evaluate issue management as a leading indicator of risk management culture. A bank with a large, aged issue population — especially repeat findings — signals that the root cause identification and remediation process is not working. The second line's role is to ensure that findings are genuinely remediated, not just administratively closed. This requires understanding what "done" looks like for each specific root cause.
